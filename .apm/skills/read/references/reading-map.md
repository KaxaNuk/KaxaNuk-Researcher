The session's premise: every belief a person holds about markets has already been written down,
tested and argued with, and research starts by finding out by whom. Investment research did not
evolve by replacement; it evolved by addition (page 48).

## Start here — the ten papers of the two timelines

### The argument, dated (page 49)

Five papers, thirty-four years. Read in order, the case for research makes itself.

1. **The claim** — 1970, Fama, *Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work*.
   Prices already reflect what is known; beating the market is luck.
2. **The counterparty** — 1979, Kahneman & Tversky, *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under
   Risk*. People are not the calculators the claim assumes: losing hurts more than winning feels
   good, and the mistake repeats.
3. **Why it must exist** — 1980, Grossman & Stiglitz, *On the Impossibility of Informationally
   Efficient Markets*. If information costs money, prices cannot be perfect: somebody has to be
   paid to make them right.
4. **Why it persists** — 1997, Shleifer & Vishny, *The Limits of Arbitrage*. Seeing the mispricing
   is not holding the trade; capital, risk and horizon run out first.
5. **Why it keeps moving** — 2004, Lo, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis*. Edges get crowded and
   decay, the environment shifts, new ones appear; nothing stays put.

Alpha has to exist. It has a source. It persists. And it keeps moving. Therefore research cannot be
a one-time effort: the edge is in the process that finds the next one, not in any single signal.

### The evidence, dated (page 50)

Five results, twelve years. Edges are real, they decay, and whether they were ever real is still
being fought over.

6. **Crowding** — 2011, Khandani & Lo, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? Evidence from
   Factors and Transactions Data*. August 2007: quant books that shared a strategy unwound together
   in three days; an ecological shock, reconstructed. Page 35 lists it as 2007, with the short
   title *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?*, and page 53 cites it as 2007.
7. **Cycles** — 2016, Daniel & Moskowitz, *Momentum Crashes*. Momentum crashes in sharp rebounds: a
   strategy works, gets crowded, breaks, and comes back.
8. **Decay** — 2016, McLean & Pontiff, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return
   Predictability?* Anomaly returns fall about 26% out of sample and about 58% once the paper is
   published.
9. **False positives** — 2016, Harvey, Liu & Zhu, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns*.
   Hundreds of factors published; most claimed findings are false once you correct for the number
   of tests. The deck gives the title from its ellipsis on; search it with the authors' names.
10. **The rebuttal** — 2023, Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, *Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?*
    Model the factors jointly and 82% replicate, in 13 themes across 93 countries.

Real. Decaying. And still contested. Nobody will settle the factor fight for you: deciding which
edges are real, and for how long, is the job, and it never finishes.

### Can anyone beat the market?

- **No — prices already know.** Holds it: Fama, 1970 (page 49, the claim). Act III, question 07,
  "If alpha is rare and fragile, should it be assumed — or must it be proven?" (page 18). Tested:
  Cowles, 1933 — put to the test, the forecasters were noise (page 9); Jensen, 1968 — most active
  managers fail to generate persistent alpha (page 17). Other side: Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, the
  counterparty (page 49); Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 — perfect efficiency is self-defeating (pages
  23 and 51). Inside the frame: a special case — a stable environment, heavy competition and slow
  change (page 51).
- **Yes — someone is paid to know.** Holds it: Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 (page 49, why it must
  exist); Pedersen, 2015, *Efficiently Inefficient* (page 23). Act III, question 09, "If nobody
  beats the market… why does anyone still pay for research?" (page 22). Tested: the deck names no
  test. Other side: Berk & Green, 2004, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets* —
  "alpha exists" is not "the client gets it" (pages 17 and 52). KaxaNuk's position, point 01
  (page 52).
- **For a while — edges crowd and move.** Holds it: Lo, 2004 (page 49, why it keeps moving). Act
  V, question 14, "So markets are rational… and emotional. What survives both — and for how long?"
  (page 32). Tested: the evidence timeline — Khandani & Lo, 2011; Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016;
  McLean & Pontiff, 2016 (page 50). Its mechanism: Farmer, 2002 (page 35). Other side: the deck
  sets no paper against Lo. Fama, 1970, is the nearest, and the frame makes it a special case, not
  a rival (page 51); KaxaNuk's own caveat is that the frame is hard to falsify, so make it
  operational (page 52).
- **Not sure yet.** No placement. Start at the claim and read the argument in order, 1970 to 2004
  (page 49).

### If there is an edge, where does it come from?

The five sources of edge of page 56, in four options: information and implementation share the
last. *Not sure yet* is typed under *Other*.

- **Paid for a risk others avoid.** Risk premia, the first source (page 56). Holds it: Ross, 1976
  (page 21). Act III, question 08, "If one beta cannot explain returns… how many risks are we being
  paid to carry?" (page 20). Measured: Fama & French, 1992 and 1993, and Carhart, 1997 — value,
  size and momentum (page 25). Other side: Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994 — the value premium
  as a mistake, not a risk (page 31).
- **A mistake others repeat.** Behavioral, the second source (page 56). Holds it: Kahneman &
  Tversky, 1979 (page 49, the counterparty). Act IV, question 12, "If the anomalies are real… who
  keeps making the mistake?" (page 28), with Shiller, 1981, and De Bondt & Thaler, 1985 (page 29).
  Tested: the deck names no test. Other side: Fama, 1970, the claim (page 49). Why it persists:
  Shleifer & Vishny, 1997 (page 49).
- **Others cannot take the other side.** Limits to arbitrage, the third source (page 56). Holds it:
  Shleifer & Vishny, 1997 (page 49, why it persists). Act IV, question 13, "If mistakes are
  obvious… why do they survive?" (page 30), with Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994 (page 31).
  Tested: the deck names no test. Other side: Fama & French, 1992 and 1993 — the value premium as a
  risk factor, where the same authors call it a mistake (pages 25 and 31).
- **Something I see or do better.** Information and implementation, the fourth and fifth sources
  (page 56). Place the half the owner's words point to; with nothing to go on, both, a line each.
  *Information*, something measured that the price has not absorbed. Holds it: Grossman & Stiglitz,
  1980 (page 49, why it must exist). Act III, question 09, "If nobody beats the market… why does
  anyone still pay for research?" (page 22). Tested: the deck names no test. Other side: Fama,
  1970, the claim, which the deck sets against Grossman & Stiglitz (pages 23 and 51); page 56 names
  Fama with this source too — assume the price already knows, and name what it has not absorbed
  (page 19). *Implementation*, the same signal with more of it kept after costs. Holds it: Grinold
  & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management* (page 41). Act VI, question 18, "If an edge is real…
  how does it become a portfolio — and what can a machine add?" (page 40). Tested: the deck names
  no test. Other side: the deck names none.
- **Not sure yet**, typed under *Other*. No placement; Shleifer & Vishny, 1997, is still *why it
  persists* for any edge (page 49).

### Are most published findings real? (page 50)

## Beliefs people type

Beliefs a newcomer often states in their own words, placed the same way. A belief not listed here
is placed from *The six acts*.

- **What goes up keeps going up — momentum.** Act IV, question 11, "If returns have structure…
  which patterns survive the cost of trading them?" (page 26). Holds it: Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993,
  *Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers* — past winners keep winning for three to twelve
  months, the anomaly that resists a risk explanation best (page 27). Tested: Carhart, 1997, as a
  factor (page 25); Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, 2013, across markets (page 37); its crashes,
  Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016 (page 50). Other side: the deck names no work that argues momentum away;
  its test is decay, McLean & Pontiff, 2016 (pages 39 and 50).
- **Cheap stocks beat expensive ones — value.** Act II, question 03, "If prices wander… what is a
  security actually worth, and how much of it should you hold?" (page 10). Holds it: Graham &
  Dodd, 1934, *Security Analysis* (page 11). Why it pays is contested: Fama & French, 1992 and
  1993, a risk factor (page 25), against Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, 1994, a mistake —
  extrapolation error (page 31). Other side: whichever of the two the owner does not hold.
- **Charts and trends show where prices go.** Act I, question 01, "Can prices be predicted at
  all?" (page 6). Holds it: Dow, 1889, and Nelson, 1903 — trends reflect human behavior (page 7).
  Tested: Cowles, 1933, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?* (page 9). Other side: Cowles,
  1933; Kendall, 1953; Samuelson, 1965 — price changes look random — and then Lo & MacKinlay,
  1988, who rejected the random walk on the data (pages 9 and 33).
- **Prices overreact, and bubbles happen.** Act IV, question 12 (page 28). Holds it: Shiller,
  1981, *Do Stock Prices Move Too Much?*; De Bondt & Thaler, 1985, *Does the Stock Market
  Overreact?* (page 29). Other side: Fama, 1970, the claim (page 49).
- **Timing the big turns is what matters.** Act V, question 14: regimes matter (page 33); Act VII,
  problem 1, regimes you can see coming — Hamilton, 1989, and Ang & Timmermann, 2012, given without
  titles (page 53). Other side: Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management* — many small
  bets beat a few big calls; one call on the market is not a strategy (page 41).
- **Big players' flows move prices.** Act V, question 15, "If markets adapt… what exactly are they
  adapting to?" (page 34). Holds it: Farmer, 2002, *Market Force, Ecology and Evolution*; Khandani
  & Lo, the August 2007 unwind (page 35). Other side: the deck names none; Act VII, problem 2, says
  the data on the crowd is thin, late or proprietary (page 53).
- **Machines will find what people miss.** Act VI, question 20, "If clean signals are rare… who
  finds them faster: humans or machines?" (page 44). Holds it: Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020, *Empirical
  Asset Pricing via Machine Learning* (page 45). Other side: Act VII, problem 3 — the binding
  constraint is economic structure and sample size, not compute — with Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz,
  2019, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning* (page 53).

## The six acts

Each act asks the question the one before could not answer, dated by the works that forced it
(page 5). The arc's 21 questions are given as the deck words them, with the page of the question;
the works are on the page after it. *Edge* is the deck's "where the edge is" line, shortened. The
edge lines are strategy-stage questions, never questions for setup.

### Act I — Prices: can prices be predicted?

Prices look random; forecasters look like noise.

**01. Can prices be predicted at all?** (page 6) — Dow, Bachelier & Nelson, *markets as signals*.

- 1889, Dow, *Wall Street Journal editorials* — a body of editorials, not one title.
- 1900, Bachelier, *Théorie de la Spéculation* — the work that changed it.
- 1903, Nelson, *The ABC of Stock Speculation*.
- Edge: if prices carry information, so does their sequence — trend, reversal and volatility
  clustering all start here.

**02. If prices carry information… can anyone read it better than the crowd?** (page 8) — Cowles,
Kendall & Samuelson, *the first scoreboard*.

- 1933, Cowles, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?* — the work that changed it.
- 1953, Kendall, *The Analysis of Economic Time-Series, Part I: Prices*.
- 1965, Samuelson, *Proof That Properly Anticipated Prices Fluctuate Randomly*.
- Edge: build the scoreboard first; a strategy not measured against a passive alternative has not
  been tested at all.

### Act II — Value and risk: what is it worth, how much to hold?

Value is a model. Risk is priced.

**03. If prices wander… what is a security actually worth, and how much of it should you hold?**
(page 10) — Graham, Dodd & Damodaran, *intrinsic value*.

- 1934, Graham & Dodd, *Security Analysis* — the work that changed it.
- 1938, Williams, *The Theory of Investment Value*.
- 1994, Damodaran, *Investment Valuation*.
- Edge: the gap between a story and a number is a hypothesis; mispricing against intrinsic value is
  the oldest edge there is.

**04. If value exists… how do we allocate capital across many bets?** (page 12) — Markowitz, *risk
comes first*.

- 1952, Markowitz, *Portfolio Selection* — the work that changed it.
- 1959, *Portfolio Selection: Efficient Diversification of Investments* — the deck names no author
  on the line; the slide is Markowitz's.
- Edge: in how you combine bets, not only in which ones you pick; correlation is a return you can
  engineer.

**05. If risk can be measured… which risk deserves a reward?** (page 14) — Sharpe & Lintner,
*pricing the risk*. Treynor and Mossin reached the same result independently; the deck lists no
work of theirs.

- 1964, Sharpe, *Capital Asset Prices* — the work that changed it.
- 1965, Lintner, *The Valuation of Risk Assets*.
- Edge: before you call it alpha, ask whether it is beta in disguise; strip market exposure first.

### Act III — Efficiency: does anyone beat the market?

Proof is on you — and research is what efficiency pays for.

**06. Does anyone actually beat the market — and if not, why would anyone try?** (page 16) —
Jensen, *measuring skill*.

- 1968, Jensen, *The Performance of Mutual Funds in the Period 1945–1964* — the work that changed
  it.
- 2004, Berk & Green, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets*.
- Edge: set the bar before you look — a risk-adjusted benchmark, not zero. Measure, then believe.

**07. If alpha is rare and fragile, should it be assumed — or must it be proven?** (page 18) —
Fama, *markets as a discipline*.

- 1970, Fama, *Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work* — the work that
  changed it, which the headline shortens to *Efficient Capital Markets*; timeline paper 1.
- 1991, *Efficient Capital Markets: II* — the deck names no author on the line; the slide is
  Fama's.
- Edge: assume the price already knows; name the information it has not absorbed, or the reason it
  cannot.

**08. If one beta cannot explain returns… how many risks are we being paid to carry?** (page 20) —
Ross, *risk is multi-dimensional*.

- 1976, Ross, *The Arbitrage Theory of Capital Asset Pricing* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: unnamed risk premia are candidate strategies; find a risk others refuse to hold and get
  paid for holding it.

**09. If nobody beats the market… why does anyone still pay for research?** (page 22) — Grossman &
Stiglitz, *the paradox that pays for research*.

- 1980, Grossman & Stiglitz, *On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets* — the work
  that changed it; timeline paper 3.
- 2015, Pedersen, *Efficiently Inefficient*.
- Edge: the wage for information others found too expensive to gather; what does it cost to know
  this, and why will the crowd not pay it?

### Act IV — Anomalies: why does the data misbehave?

Returns have structure. Mispricing has a mechanism.

**10. If markets are efficient… why does the evidence refuse to behave?** (page 24) — Fama, French
& Carhart, *empirical reality*.

- 1992, Fama & French, *The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns*.
- 1993, Fama & French, *Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds* — the work that
  changed it, which the headline gives as *Common Risk Factors in Stocks and Bonds*.
- 1997, Carhart, *On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance*.
- Edge: known factors are the hurdle, not the idea; a new edge survives value, size and momentum
  stripped out.

**11. If returns have structure… which patterns survive the cost of trading them?** (page 26) —
Jegadeesh & Titman · Amihud, *momentum meets liquidity*.

- 1993, Jegadeesh & Titman, *Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers* — the work that changed
  it.
- 2002, Amihud, *Illiquidity and Stock Returns: Cross-Section and Time-Series Effects*.
- Edge: friction is edge; illiquidity is priced, and the capacity to trade it decides who collects.

**12. If the anomalies are real… who keeps making the mistake?** (page 28) — Kahneman, Tversky,
Thaler & Shiller, *human behavior*.

- 1979, Kahneman & Tversky, *Prospect Theory* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 2, which
  page 49 gives in full as *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk*.
- 1981, Shiller, *Do Stock Prices Move Too Much?*
- 1985, De Bondt & Thaler, *Does the Stock Market Overreact?*
- Edge: systematic human error is the most durable mispricing; which bias makes the counterparty
  sell to you?

**13. If mistakes are obvious… why do they survive?** (page 30) — Shleifer & Vishny, *limits to
arbitrage*.

- 1994, Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny, *Contrarian Investment, Extrapolation, and Risk*.
- 1997, Shleifer & Vishny, *The Limits of Arbitrage* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 4.
- Edge: ask who cannot take the other side — constrained capital, career risk, forced sellers.

### Act V — Discipline: do edges last?

Edges decay and the bar rises.

**14. So markets are rational… and emotional. What survives both — and for how long?** (page 32)
— Lo, *markets adapt*.

- 1988, Lo & MacKinlay, *Stock Market Prices Do Not Follow Random Walks*.
- 2004, Lo, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis* — the work that changed it; timeline paper 5.
- 2017, Lo, *Adaptive Markets: Financial Evolution at the Speed of Thought*.
- Edge: every edge decays; build in the signal that the regime has changed, and be ready to retire
  the strategy.

**15. If markets adapt… what exactly are they adapting to?** (page 34) — Farmer · Arthur · Soros,
*markets are ecologies*.

- 1987, Soros, *The Alchemy of Finance*.
- 1997, Arthur et al., *Asset Pricing under Endogenous Expectations in an Artificial Stock Market*.
- 2002, Farmer, *Market Force, Ecology and Evolution* — the work that changed it.
- 2007, Khandani & Lo, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?* — timeline paper 6, which page
  50 dates 2011 with the long title.
- Edge: map the ecology before you enter it — who else runs this, with how much capital, on what
  horizon.

**16. If alpha decays… what does durable research look like?** (page 36) — Asness, *research
discipline in practice*.

- 1997, *The Interaction of Value and Momentum Strategies* — the deck names no author on the line;
  the slide is Asness's.
- 2013, Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen, *Value and Momentum Everywhere* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: combine premia that fail at different times; robustness across markets beats a perfect fit
  in one.

**17. If robustness beats intuition… how many backtests did it take to find yours?** (page 38) —
Harvey, Liu & Zhu · McLean & Pontiff, *the bar rises*.

- 2016, Harvey, Liu & Zhu, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns* — the work that changed
  it; timeline paper 9.
- 2016, McLean & Pontiff, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?* — timeline
  paper 8.
- 2019, Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of Machine Learning*.
- The page adds that how many were ever real is still contested — Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023,
  timeline paper 10.
- Edge: count your trials; an idea has to be justified before the backtest, which can only fail to
  reject it.

### Act VI — Engineering: how does an idea become a book?

Alpha is residualized, scaled and learned.

**18. If an edge is real… how does it become a portfolio — and what can a machine add?** (page 40)
— Rosenberg · Grinold & Kahn, *from theory to the desk*.

- 1974, Rosenberg, *Extra-Market Components of Covariance in Security Returns*.
- 1989, Grinold, *The Fundamental Law of Active Management*.
- 1995, Grinold & Kahn, *Active Portfolio Management* — the work that changed it.
- Edge: edge scales with breadth; how many independent bets a year does the idea give you?

**19. If everything has exposure… what is actually yours?** (page 42) — Paleologo, *from ideas to
portfolios*.

- 2021, Paleologo, *Advanced Portfolio Management* — the work that changed it.
- 2025, *The Elements of Quantitative Investing* — the deck names no author on the line; the slide
  is Paleologo's.
- Edge: residualize; what is left after known factors is the only part anyone will pay you for.

**20. If clean signals are rare… who finds them faster: humans or machines?** (page 44) — Gu, Kelly
& Xiu · Dixon et al., *machine learning*.

- 2020, Gu, Kelly & Xiu, *Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning* — the work that changed it.
- 2020, Dixon, Halperin & Bilokon, *Machine Learning in Finance: From Theory to Practice*.
- 2023, Kelly & Xiu, *Financial Machine Learning*.
- Edge: let the model learn structure you cannot write down, only where you can say why it should
  exist.

**21. If learning systems can model perception and decision together, how should we design
investment research going forward?** (page 46) — Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, *investment systems*.

- 2022, Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, *Quant 4.0: Engineering Quantitative Investment with Automated,
  Explainable and Knowledge-driven AI* — the work that changed it, which the headline shortens to
  *Quant 4.0*.
- Edge: the durable edge is the system that keeps generating and retiring hypotheses, not any
  single one of them.

## Nothing is discarded (page 51)

Inside the Adaptive Markets Hypothesis — Lo, 2004: efficiency depends on the ecology. Each theory
keeps its job; the frame is what lets them stop contradicting each other.

- **Efficient markets** — Fama, 1970. A special case: a stable environment, heavy competition and
  slow change.
- **Behavioral finance** — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler. Where the maladaptive heuristics come from:
  shortcuts that worked in another environment.
- **Limits to arbitrage** — Shleifer & Vishny, 1997. The mechanism that lets inefficiencies last:
  capital, risk and horizon constraints.
- **Costly information** — Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980. The equilibrium: markets cannot be perfectly
  efficient, or nobody would pay for information.
- **Efficiently inefficient** — Pedersen, 2015. The practical version: just enough inefficiency to
  pay the active managers who compete it away.
- **Market ecology** — Farmer · Santa Fe complexity, 1997–2002. The formal version: strategies as
  species, with interactions you can model.
- **Reflexivity** — Soros, 1987. Feedback between participants and prices, part of what changes the
  environment.

## Why we say alpha has to exist — KaxaNuk's position (page 52)

1. **Alpha has to exist.** Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 — if information is costly, someone is paid to
   gather it.
2. **It has a source.** Behavioral finance — heuristics that worked in another environment keep
   firing in this one.
3. **It persists.** Limits to arbitrage — capital, risk and horizon constraints on the people who
   see it.
4. **It keeps moving.** Adaptive markets — edges crowd and decay, the environment shifts, new ones
   appear.

Said before anyone else does:

- **The frame is hard to falsify.** So make it operational: measure crowding, capacity, flows and
  the participant mix.
- **Grossman–Stiglitz is the stronger claim.** Use it for why alpha cannot go to zero; use Lo for
  how it moves.
- **Berk & Green cut the other way.** "Alpha exists" is not "the client gets it": capital chases
  skill until the net return is gone. Say which claim you are making.

The edge is in the process, not in any single signal. What KaxaNuk builds: research infrastructure
as the adaptive capacity, not a fixed strategy. The research process is discretionary at design and
systematic at scale; scientific principles are what connect the two (page 54).

## Where alpha comes from — Section 02 (pages 55 to 60)

Each stop on the arc points to a source of edge, and the deck keeps five that hold up under
scrutiny (page 55). Everything here is a hint: a question to put to an idea, which the owner may
answer, edit, skip or refuse, never a position to adopt. These pages give surnames only; the works
behind them are the ones *The six acts* gives under the question named, and a name the acts give no
work for is proposed by no title.

### Five sources of edge (page 56)

An idea with no named source of edge is only a pattern: say which source it draws on, and why that
door is still open, before testing anything.

1. **Risk premia** — being paid for carrying a risk others will not. *Ask:* what risk am I
   carrying, and who pays me to carry it? Sharpe · Ross · Fama & French — questions 05, 08 and 10.
2. **Behavioral** — people making the same error, in the same direction, over and over. *Ask:*
   which bias leads the other side to sell to me? Kahneman & Tversky · Thaler · Shiller — question
   12.
3. **Limits to arbitrage** — those who know better are unable to act on it. *Ask:* who cannot take
   the other side, and why? Shleifer & Vishny · Amihud — questions 13 and 11.
4. **Information** — measuring something the price has not yet taken in. *Ask:* what can I see
   that the price cannot, and why am I the one who sees it? Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama · Gu, Kelly &
   Xiu — questions 09, 07 and 20.
5. **Implementation** — the same signal, with more of it kept after costs. *Ask:* can I collect a
   known edge better than others do? Markowitz · Grinold & Kahn · Paleologo — questions 04, 18 and
   19.

`interview` offers them as four options, the last two together as *Something I see or do better*;
*Where a belief sits* places each.

### Where ideas come from, and how each fools you (page 57)

Six places to look, each with its trap. The page names no work.

1. **Published anomalies** — the factor zoo: hundreds of documented patterns, most with a paper
   behind them. *Trap:* most are noise; prefer an idea with a mechanism that replicated out of
   sample.
2. **Market structure** — index rebalances, option expiries, month-end flows, forced sellers.
   *Trap:* crowded and small; check capacity before getting attached.
3. **Behavioral patterns** — slow reaction to news, overreaction to stories, the disposition
   effect. *Trap:* name the bias and who holds it, or it is only a story.
4. **Frictions** — illiquidity, funding constraints, limits on short sales. *Trap:* the premium
   exists; what is being tested is the cost of collecting it.
5. **Your own data** — something you can measure that others do not, or measure better. *Trap:* a
   new dataset generates hypotheses; it is not one.
6. **Combining known premia** — value with momentum, trend with liquidity, carry with trend.
   *Trap:* the mix is an edge only if its parts fail at different times.

### Seven questions before any backtest (page 58)

Each with the names the deck sets beside it. The page's test: if the seven answers do not fit in
one paragraph, the idea is not yet a hypothesis.

1. What makes this edge exist? — Grossman & Stiglitz · Fama.
2. Who is on the other side, and what keeps them on the losing end? — Kahneman & Tversky ·
   Shleifer & Vishny.
3. Paid risk or mispricing — and which one am I setting out to own? — Ross · Fama & French.
4. How many independent bets a year does it offer? — Grinold & Kahn.
5. Does it last after costs, and how much capital before it stops working? — Amihud · Paleologo.
6. When does it break, and would I stay in through the break? — Lo · Shiller.
7. How many ideas came before this one? — Harvey, Liu & Zhu · McLean & Pontiff.

### An idea's anatomy (page 59)

A belief is one sentence; a hypothesis also names its edge, the other side, its test, and what
would make you drop it. Five parts:

- **The claim** — one sentence, in the present tense, about a relationship that can be measured.
- **The source of edge** — which of the five it draws on, and why that door should still be open.
- **The other side** — who sells to you, and what keeps them doing so.
- **The test** — the measurement that settles it, chosen before looking at the data.
- **The kill switch** — the result that ends the idea, rather than sending it back for tuning.

The deck fills each part for one worked idea, trend in liquid assets. It shows the shape — a claim
a measurement can settle, a kill switch read off sub-periods net of costs — and is not a claim to
adopt.

### Discussion (page 60)

Three questions the deck leaves open, to put to any belief:

- Whose paper does it contradict, and can the owner live with that?
- If alpha decays, how long would the strategy they want to build last?
- What evidence would change their mind before four months go into it?

`interview` asks the last two its own way, as the belief's shelf life and what would change the
owner's mind; the first is the other side it offers to find.
