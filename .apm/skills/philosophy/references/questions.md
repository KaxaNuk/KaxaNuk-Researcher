# Question bank — `philosophy`

> **This file is `references/questions.md` in the `philosophy` skill's folder**, read whole before
> the first question.
>
> - It is written in English and said in the owner's language, the one *How it speaks* names in
>   `RESEARCHER.md`. Everything said or shown is translated: the welcome and the explainers, every
>   *Ask*, *Plain*, *Example* and *Teaches* line, option labels and their descriptions, the closing
>   line of every open question, the checkpoints and the retake's lines.
> - A work keeps its year, authors and title exactly as the reading map gives them, untranslated.
> - Headers are given as English (Spanish), twelve characters at most. Any other language
>   translates the English.
> - IDs are stable and never renumbered; a retake compares by ID. O is the opening, Q a question,
>   S a stance pick, C the closing. IDs stay in English in every file, with the other labels the
>   skills parse: the level names, *Round*, *(round N)*, *kept*, *changed*, *new*, *still open*,
>   and the statuses *not sure yet*, *skipped* and *explained*.
>
> **Works are leads, never sources.**
>
> - Every work named here comes from the reading map, `references/reading-map.md` in the `read`
>   skill's folder, with the year, authors, title and page the map gives it.
> - *p. N* is the page of the bootcamp deck the map gives for that work or that line. It locates
>   the lead; it is never a page of the paper.
> - A paper's finding is said as the map sums it up, and as a lead to read, never as a settled
>   fact. Nothing is claimed on a work until the owner's `Knowledge/` holds a note on it.
> - Where `Knowledge/` already holds a note on the work, found as the map's *Match before
>   proposing* says, name and link the note instead.
> - Where the map names no other side, say so. Nothing is proposed from memory.
> - An *Edge* line of the map's *The six acts* is put to the owner at Researching only, as a
>   question about how they invest, never as a strategy's rule: Q5 is the one. Below Researching
>   none is asked, quoted or taught — the calibration included.
> - The hints of the map's *Where alpha comes from* — the five sources of edge, the seven
>   questions, an idea's anatomy, the discussion — are put to the owner as questions about how they
>   invest, never as a strategy's rules.
> - Without the reading map, no work is named at all: section 9.
>
> **No positions, no advice.**
>
> - No question asks the owner to adopt a view, and none asks how KaxaNuk invests.
> - No line names a product or a ticker, proposes an allocation or a benchmark, or computes a
>   return. Nothing says buy, sell or hold.
> - Examples are what other people say, never a recommendation.
> - A **personal benchmark** is the owner's yardstick for their own money. A strategy's benchmark
>   is chosen later, at part E, and is a different thing. A question here on a yardstick, a stop or
>   how long to hold asks about the owner's own habit, never a strategy's rule.
>
> **No answer is right or wrong. Nothing is scored.**
>
> - *Teaches* lines are said as written: orientation from the map, an idea to check.
> - They are never improvised and never a verdict on the answer.
> - Only what the owner types reaches `Philosophy/`. A pick, an example, a placement and every line
>   of this file stay in chat.

## How a card reads

- **ID.** It stays the same across levels and rounds, and a retake compares answers by it.
- **Asked at.** Starter (beginner), Building (intermediate) or Researching (advanced). A level
  with no wording of its own uses the next simpler one.
- **Ask.** The question, as worded at that level. Q3, Q6, Q8 and Q14 at Starter are worded for
  someone who does not invest yet as much as for someone who does.
- **Plain.** Said on *explain*: one plain line, then the question again in its plainest form — its
  Starter wording, or, for a question Starter does not ask, the question in *Plain*.
- **Example.** Said on *example*: two or three answers other people give, labelled as examples.
  - An example is never written.
  - An owner who says one again in their own words has typed it. A number, or *that one*, is a
    pick, not typed words.
- **Teaches.** One idea, said after any answer, *not sure yet* and *skip* included.
  - Starter names no paper.
  - Building names at most one work, as a lead.
  - Researching gives the placement and the other side.
- **Lands under.** The heading of `Philosophy/HOW-I-INVEST.md` where a typed answer goes.
- **Behind it.** Shown at Researching only, or when the owner asks *show me the paper*.
  - These are the map's leads.
  - *A gap* means the map holds no work, and the gap is said out loud.
- **Presses on.** At Researching and on every retake: quote the owner's own line in
  `HOW-I-INVEST.md` that this question tests, if one clearly does. Never guess one.

## How every question is asked

- **Open questions are asked in chat**, because only typed words reach the file.
  - One question per message. At Building and Researching, the owner may ask for a whole block at
    once.
  - Every open question ends with the same line:
    > *Answer in your words — one line is enough. Or say: example · explain · not sure yet · skip.*
- **Pick questions are asked with the question tool** (`AskUserQuestion` in Claude Code): at most
  four options, plus *Other*.
  - Without a question tool (Codex, Gemini), the question is one chat message, with the options
    numbered and *Other — your own words* last.
- **After every pick**, one optional line: *Want to say that in your own words? One line, or
  skip.* Only that typed line reaches the file.
- **The escapes**, available on every question, are recognised in the owner's language and in
  their own wording too. A message that is only an escape is the escape and writes nothing; a
  message with more than that is a typed answer. When it could be either, ask once.

## 1 · Opening — every level, plain words for everyone

### O1 · Why you invest
- *Asked at:* every level, in chat.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Ask:** Why do you invest, or why do you want to start? What would it change for you?
- **Plain:** This is about you, not about markets. Any reason counts.
- **Example:**
  - "So inflation doesn't eat my savings."
  - "To depend less on my salary one day."
  - "Markets fascinate me."
  - "It's my job."
- **Teaches:** Why you invest shapes almost everything after it: how long you can wait, how big a
  fall you can live with, and what *doing well* means for you.
- **Behind it:** none. This one is about you.

### O2 · What you already believe
- *Asked at:* every level, in chat.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Ask:** What do you already believe about money and markets? Anything at all — a hunch, or
  something you heard and aren't sure of.
- **Plain:** Everyone has a few, and they don't have to be right. "It's a casino" counts.
- **If nothing comes** — *not sure yet*, or *example* — ask one multi-select tool question,
  `Hunches` (`Intuiciones`), once. The hunches are six of the seven beliefs of the map's *Beliefs
  people type*, in plain words, and one from its *Where a belief sits*, *No — prices already
  know*, said as the first below.
  - First call — three hunches and a fourth option, *More hunches*:
    - *Nobody can do better than the market as a whole*
    - *What goes up keeps going up*
    - *Bargain shares beat pricey ones*
  - On *More hunches*, a second call with the other four:
    - *Charts show where prices go*
    - *Prices overreact, and bubbles happen*
    - *Timing the big turns is what matters*
    - *Machines will find what people miss*
  - Without a question tool: one numbered list of all seven.
  - Picking none keeps *not sure yet*, a complete answer. A belief the owner types under *Other*,
    or later, is placed the same way — the belief that big players' money moves prices among them,
    the map's *Big players' flows move prices*, which the map places though the hunches leave it
    out.
  - Then ask: *Want to say that belief in your own words — and why, if you like? One line, or
    skip.* Only that line is written, never the hunch; it is the O2 line C1 quotes.
- **Teaches:** Almost every belief about markets has already been written down, tested and argued
  over by someone. Reading is how you find out by whom, and whether it held up.
- **Placement,** said after O3, once the level is known. It is never written to any file.
  - **Starter:** one plain line, no paper. *People have argued about this for a long time, and
    there's a well-known study on it; I'll suggest it at the end.*
  - **Building:** the work that holds the belief, and its other side, as *Beliefs people type* or
    *Where a belief sits* names them, as leads.
  - **Researching:** the act and the arc's question, the work that holds it, who tested it, and its
    other side as the map names it.
  - A belief neither entry lists is placed by the arc question whose works or core ideas name it,
    in *The six acts*. If the map names no other side, or places nothing, say so. Nothing is placed
    for *not sure yet*.

### O3 · How much you know
- *Asked at:* every level, as a single-select tool question.
- *Lands under:* nothing. The level goes only into the round file's first line.
- Say first: *This only sets how I word the next questions. Say "simpler" or "harder" at any
  time.*
- Header: `Experience` (`Experiencia`).
- **Ask:** How much do you know about investing today?
- **Options:**

| Option | Description shown | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Just starting* | I save, maybe have a pension, but have never chosen an investment myself, or only a little. If *index funds*, *diversification* and *fees* are new words to you, this is your level. | Starter |
| *Some experience* | I've bought funds, shares or crypto; index funds, diversification and fees are familiar. | Building |
| *I research* | I test ideas with data, read papers on markets, or work in finance. | Researching |
| *Not sure — help me place myself* | Pick the question you could answer today; nothing is checked. | the calibration below |

- **The suggested option.** One option is marked *(suggested)*, by the order the skill's *step 4*
  gives; it is never picked for the owner. On a retake, the last level is marked *(current)*.
- **Calibration, only after *Not sure*.**
  - Header: `Which fits?` (`¿Cuál va?`).
  - **Ask:** Which of these could you answer today, in a sentence? Pick one; nothing is checked.
  - **Options**, a short label and the question in its description:

| Label | Description shown | Level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Doing nothing* | Where would my money be if I did nothing clever with it? | Starter |
| *Comparing* | What should I compare my results against, and why that one? | Building |
| *Luck or skill* | How would I tell a good result of mine from luck, over years and after costs? | Researching |
| *None yet* | None of these, yet. | Starter, with the *Example* under every question |

  - Say the result, and let the owner change it.

## 2 · The idea the rest builds on (said once, after O3, never asked)

Each version is neutral: it settles nothing the owner answers later, *can anyone do better than
the market?* least of all. The purpose line is the same in all three, and the only one.

### Starter

> One idea first, because everything after builds on it.
>
> Everyone who saves already has a yardstick, even without a name for it: what the money would do
> if you did nothing clever with it. It might sit in the bank, in a pension, or in a fund that buys
> a little of every big company, so you don't have to choose. I'll call it your **personal
> benchmark**.
>
> Every investment choice after that is a bet that you can do better than it, after fees. A
> well-known study of professional funds suggests it's hard; whether it can be done is one of the
> things you'll decide for yourself.
>
> Either way, that's what a **process** is for:
> - write down what you believe, and why;
> - say what you'll compare it with;
> - test it before money is at stake;
> - decide what you'll do before you feel it.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick.
>
> One honest caveat: none of the studies I start from shows that a process makes more money. A
> process is how you find out whether a result is yours, or luck.

If the owner asks which study: the map's Jensen, 1968, *The Performance of Mutual Funds in the
Period 1945–1964*, which it sums up as most active managers failing to generate persistent alpha
(p. 17). That is a lead to read, not something cited yet. *The studies I start from* are the
reading map's.

### Building

> Everything below is measured against one thing: your **personal benchmark**, what your money
> would do with no decisions, tied to your goal. Whether anyone can do better than it, after costs,
> is one of the questions you'll answer — the reading map holds both sides.
>
> A **process** is how you find out whether you did: what you believe written down, a yardstick
> chosen in advance, ideas tested before money is at stake.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick. Nothing on the
> reading map shows that a process makes more money; it's how you find out whether a result is
> yours, or luck.

### Researching

> These questions follow the papers of the map's two timelines, each named by what it is about:
> - the claim (Fama, 1970);
> - the counterparty (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979);
> - the cost of knowing (Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980);
> - the limits of arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997);
> - adaptation (Lo, 2004);
> - then the evidence from 2011 to 2023: crowding, cycles, decay, false positives, and the
>   rebuttal (pp. 49–50).
>
> Your **personal benchmark** is your own yardstick, tied to your goal; a strategy's benchmark is
> chosen later, at part E, and is a different thing. Whether anyone does better than yours after
> costs is a question you'll answer, not one I assume.
>
> I'm here so you can make better investment decisions, by your own yardstick. The map holds no
> paper showing that a process makes more money. A process is how you find out whether a result is
> yours, or luck.

changes the wording of a question the level asks; it never adds one the level does not ask.

## 4 · The questions

### Your scoreboard

#### Q2 · Your goal
- *Asked at:* every level; at Starter, only when O1 named no goal or no date. A Starter round that
  does not ask it gives it no line, and no retake counts it as never reached.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** a one-line follow-up to O1, first in the block: *You said "<O1>". And by when?* —
    *What for, and by when?* when O1 named no goal, without the quote when it has no typed words.
  - **Building:** What is this money for, by when, and what happens if it isn't there in time?
  - **Researching:** What do you want this money to do for you, and by when? Which yardstick
    follows from that goal?
- **Plain:** A goal with a date, big or small.
- **Example:**
  - "A deposit for a flat in five years."
  - "An income from age 60."
  - "Nothing specific yet; I want it to grow."
- **Teaches:** The date matters as much as the goal. Money you need in two years has less time to
  wait out a bad year than money for twenty.
- **Behind it:** *a gap*. The map holds no work on goals or goal-based benchmarks. Say so, and
  propose nothing.

#### Q1 · Your personal benchmark
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **Why I invest**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** Suppose you never chose an investment on purpose. Where would your savings sit — a
    bank account, a pension, cash at home? If you have none yet, where would you put the first?
  - **Building:** What do you compare your results against — a savings rate, a market index, a
    mix? Why that one?
  - **Researching:** If you did nothing clever with this money, where would it be? Name that
    benchmark and the risk it carries, and why it's the right comparison.
- **Plain:** Imagine you never pick an investment on purpose. Where does the money sit: a bank
  account, a pension, lending to the government (bonds), cash, a fund that buys a little of every
  big company?
- **Example:**
  - "In my savings account."
  - "In my company pension fund."
  - "In a fund that buys a little of every big company."
- **Teaches:**
  - **Every level:** That is your personal benchmark. Every choice from here is a bet you can do
    better than it. You choose it, and you can change it.
  - **After *not sure yet* or *skip*, instead:** Wherever your money sits when you do nothing
    clever with it is your personal benchmark. Every choice from here is a bet you can do better
    than it.
  - **Building adds:** A personal benchmark isn't a strategy's benchmark; a strategy picks its own
    later. Jensen (1968) measured fund managers — a lead to read.
  - **Researching adds, as well:** Cowles (1933) put forecasters to the test — a lead to read too.
- **Behind it:**
  - Cowles, 1933, *Can Stock Market Forecasters Forecast?* (Act I, question 02; p. 9).
  - Jensen, 1968, *The Performance of Mutual Funds in the Period 1945–1964* (Act III, question 06;
    p. 17).
  - Fama, 1970, *Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work* (Start here 1,
    p. 49).
  - **Other side:** Berk & Green, 2004, *Mutual Fund Flows and Performance in Rational Markets*:
    "alpha exists" is not "the client gets it" (pp. 17, 52).
- **Never:** a product, a ticker, an allocation or a computed return.

#### Q3 · How you would know
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** If you start investing, how would you know in a few years whether your choices did
    better than leaving the money where it was?
  - **Building:** Over how many years, and after which costs (fees, taxes, trading), would you say
    you beat your personal benchmark?
  - **Researching:** How would you know you beat it — over how many years, against which risk,
    after which costs? How would that look different from a lucky run of the same length?
- **Plain:** Compare with your answer about where the money would sit anyway.
- **Example:**
  - "If after five years I have more than my savings account would have paid, after fees and
    taxes."
  - "I don't know yet."
- **Teaches:** One good year proves little; luck can do that. Fees and taxes come out before you
  compare, not after.
- **Behind it:**
  - Sharpe, 1964, *Capital Asset Prices*, under Act II, question 05: *If risk can be measured…
    which risk deserves a reward?* (p. 14).
  - Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980, *On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets* (Start
    here 3, p. 49).
  - Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, *Is There a Replication Crisis in Finance?* (Start here 10,
    p. 50).

### Your edge

#### Q4 · The other side
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building:** When you buy, someone sells to you. Why would you be right and they be wrong?
  - **Researching:** Why would you know something the price doesn't already show? Who is on the
    other side of your trade, and what keeps them on the losing end?
- **Plain:** Every trade has a buyer and a seller, and both think they're right.
- **Example:**
  - "I can wait ten years; many sellers need their money next month."
  - "I don't know — that's what I want to find out."
- **Teaches:** An idea with no answer to *who is on the other side?* is only a pattern. The map's
  *Five sources of edge* (p. 56) names five places an edge can come from — hints to check, not
  rules:
  - being paid for a risk others avoid;
  - others repeating a mistake;
  - others who can't trade against it;
  - seeing something the price hasn't taken in;
  - doing it better, after costs.
- **Behind it:**
  - Fama, 1970, and Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (Start here 1–2, p. 49).
  - Shleifer & Vishny, 1997 (Start here 4, p. 49).
  - *Five sources of edge* (p. 56).
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 2 (p. 58).

#### Q5 · What it costs to know
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Ask:** What does it cost you to know what you know — in time, data and tools? Is your edge
  worth more than that, after costs?
- **Plain:** What do you spend (hours, subscriptions, software) to find your ideas, and do they pay
  you back more than that?
- **Example:** "Two evenings a week and a data subscription; I don't know yet whether it pays."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Grossman & Stiglitz (1980) like this: if information costs money,
  prices cannot be perfect; somebody has to be paid to make them right. Its practical version,
  Pedersen (2015): just enough inefficiency to pay the active managers who compete it away. Both
  are leads to read, not settled facts.
- **Behind it:**
  - Grossman & Stiglitz, 1980 (Start here 3, p. 49). The question is Act III, question 09's edge
    line, put to the owner (p. 22).
  - Pedersen, 2015, *Efficiently Inefficient* (p. 23; *Nothing is discarded*, p. 51).
  - **Other side:** Fama, 1970 (pp. 23, 51); Berk & Green, 2004 (p. 52).

### You

#### Q6 · When it loses
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** Imagine you put 1,000 into something and a month later it's worth 800. What would
    you do? Would you decide that now, or only when it happens?
  - **Building and Researching:** When an idea loses money, what do you do? And did you decide
    that before it happened?
- **Plain:** Most people have never decided this in advance. That's the point of asking.
- **Example:**
  - "Sell, so it stops hurting."
  - "Wait; I planned to hold for years."
  - "Buy more, if I still believe in it."
- **Teaches:** Here's an idea from a well-known study, worth testing on yourself: losing tends to
  hurt more than winning feels good, so a choice made in the moment is often not the one you'd
  make on a calm day. Deciding beforehand is the protection.
  - **Building adds:** the lead is Kahneman & Tversky (1979).
- **Behind it:** Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, *Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk*
  (Start here 2, p. 49).

#### Q7 · How long you can hold
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building:** How long could you hold an idea you still believe in while it loses, and what
    would make you stop?
  - **Researching:** The same, and then: which runs out first — money, patience, or time?
- **Plain** (the plainest form): If you still believed in an investment but it kept losing, how
  long could you wait? What would make you give up?
- **Example:**
  - "A year; after that I'd need the money."
  - "As long as the reason I bought is still true."
- **Teaches:** Being right too late can cost as much as being wrong. Money, patience or time can
  run out before an idea pays.
  - **Building adds:** The map sums up Shleifer & Vishny (1997) like this: seeing the mispricing
    is not holding the trade; capital, risk and horizon run out first. A lead to read.
- **Behind it:**
  - Shleifer & Vishny, 1997, *The Limits of Arbitrage* (Start here 4, p. 49).
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 6: "When does it break, and would I stay in
    through the break?" (p. 58).

#### Q8 · Fear and excitement
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **What I have learned**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** When you hear about a market crash on the news, or a friend brags about a big
    win, what do you feel like doing? If it has never happened to you, what do you think you'd
    do?
  - **Building:** When you feel fear or excitement about a market, what do you do with it?
  - **Researching:** The same, and then: in which market did that reflex serve you?
- **Plain:** Think of a time a headline made you want to act right away.
- **Example:**
  - "I check prices all day and sometimes sell."
  - "I buy what friends buy."
  - "I switch off the news."
- **Teaches:** No habit here is wrong; knowing yours is the point. A reflex that served you in one
  setting can mislead you in another, and a plan written on a calm day lets you act on the plan
  instead.
  - **Building adds:** The map's *Nothing is discarded* gives that idea to behavioral finance —
    Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler: shortcuts that worked in another environment (p. 51). A lead to
    read.
- **Behind it:**
  - Kahneman & Tversky, 1979 (Start here 2, p. 49).
  - *Nothing is discarded*, its behavioral finance entry — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler: shortcuts
    that worked in another environment (p. 51). It names no work of Thaler's, so none is proposed.

### The world moves

#### Q9 · A very different market
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Ask:** Would your idea have worked in a very different market — a crash, a boom, rates rising?
  In which state does it break?
- **Plain:** Picture the worst year you can remember in markets. Would your idea have survived it?
- **Example:** "Probably not in a crash; it needs rising prices."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Lo (2004) like this: edges get crowded and decay, the environment
  shifts, new ones appear. A lead to read, not a settled fact. An idea that worked in only one
  kind of market may be a story about that market.
- **Behind it:**
  - Lo, 2004, *The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis* (Start here 5, p. 49).
  - Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016, *Momentum Crashes* (Start here 7, p. 50).
  - Act VII, problem 1: regimes you can see coming (p. 53). It names Hamilton (1989) and Ang &
    Timmermann (2012) without titles, so neither is proposed.

#### Q10 · Once everybody knows
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What I believe about markets**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building:** What happens to an idea once everybody knows it, or everybody holds it?
  - **Researching:** The same, and then: how much of it is left, and how long would it last?
- **Plain:** If a trick for beating the market were in every newspaper, would it still work?
- **Example:**
  - "It stops working, because everybody's already in."
  - "It keeps working if the reason behind it doesn't change."
- **Teaches:**
  - **The plain form:** An idea many people know may get priced in, and a crowded one can fall
    hard when everyone sells at once — two ideas to check, not facts.
  - **Building and Researching:** The map sums up McLean & Pontiff (2016) like this: anomaly
    returns roughly halve after publication (p. 39). A lead to read, not a settled fact.
  - **Researching adds:** And Khandani & Lo (2011), as the map sums it up: quant books that shared
    a strategy unwound together in three days. A lead to read too.
- **Behind it:**
  - McLean & Pontiff, 2016, *Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?* (Start
    here 8, p. 50).
  - Khandani & Lo, 2011, *What Happened to the Quants in August 2007? Evidence from Factors and
    Transactions Data* (Start here 6, p. 50).
  - *Discussion*, its second question: how long a strategy would last if alpha decays (p. 60).

#### Q11 · The turn, or the conditions
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask:** When the trend changes, do you act on the turn itself, or on the conditions that make a
  turn costly?
- **Plain:** Do you move when a trend turns, or do you watch for the signs that a turn would hurt?
- **Example:**
  - "I move when it turns."
  - "I cut risk after a big fall, before any turn."
- **Teaches:** The map sums up Daniel & Moskowitz (2016) like this: momentum crashes in sharp
  rebounds — a strategy works, gets crowded, breaks, and comes back. A lead to read, not a settled
  fact. If it holds, the state before a turn may matter as much as the turn.
- **Behind it:**
  - Daniel & Moskowitz, 2016 (Start here 7, p. 50).
  - **Other side, on timing:** Grinold & Kahn, 1995, *Active Portfolio Management*: many small
    bets beat a few big calls (p. 41), as *Beliefs people type* gives it.

### Your proof

#### Q12 · How many you tried
- *Asked at:* Building and Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Building:** How many ideas did you try before the one you keep?
  - **Researching:** The same, and then: had you tried twice as many, would you trust the one you
    kept any less?
- **Plain:** Toss enough coins and one will land heads ten times in a row.
- **Example:**
  - "Just one."
  - "Dozens; I never counted."
- **Teaches:** The more ideas you try, the likelier one looks good by luck alone, so the bar rises
  for the one you keep.
  - **Building adds:** the map's lead on this is Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016).
- **Behind it:**
  - Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016, *… and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns* (Start here 9, p. 50;
    Act V, question 17, p. 38).
  - *Seven questions before any backtest*, question 7: "How many ideas came before this one?"
    (p. 58).

#### Q13 · Luck or skill
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **What would change my mind**.
- **Ask:** What would convince you that a good result of yours was luck?
- **Plain:** If a friend made money last year, how would you tell whether it was skill?
- **Example:**
  - "If it stopped working the year after I started using it."
  - "Nothing — and that worries me."
- **Teaches:** If nothing could convince you, the result can't be tested, and what can't be tested
  can't be improved.
- **Behind it:** Harvey, Liu & Zhu, 2016, against Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen, 2023, *Is There a
  Replication Crisis in Finance?* Still contested (*Who argues with whom*; p. 50).

#### Q14 · Written down
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** If you invest, or when you start, would you write down your rules — when to buy,
    how much, when to sell — clearly enough that a friend could follow them and do exactly the
    same?
  - **Building:** Is your rule written down so that someone else would get the same answer?
  - **Researching:** Is it written down before the test, so that someone else would get the same
    answer?
- **Plain:** A recipe someone else can cook is a rule. "A pinch of this" is not.
- **Example:**
  - "No, it's in my head."
  - "I buy the same fund every month, whatever happens."
- **Teaches:** A rule that gives two answers when you run it twice isn't a rule yet. Writing it
  down is the first step of a process, and the cheapest.
- **Behind it:**
  - **Starter and Building:** no paper.
  - **Researching:** Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz, 2019, *A Backtesting Protocol in the Era of
    Machine Learning* (under Act V, question 17, p. 38).
  - **Researching:** *Seven questions before any backtest*, the page's test: "if the seven answers
    do not fit in one paragraph, the idea is not yet a hypothesis" (p. 58).

### Your process

#### Q15 · A decision you'd make differently
- *Asked at:* every level.
- *Lands under:* **What I have learned**.
- **Ask, by level:**
  - **Starter:** Think of a money decision you'd make differently today. What happened, and what
    would you do now?
  - **Building and Researching:** Which decision did you get wrong because you had no process?
    What would one have changed?
- **Plain:** Any money decision counts: a purchase, a loan, an investment, or one you didn't make.
- **Example:** "I bought what a friend recommended and sold in a panic. Now I'd decide when to sell
  before buying."
- **Teaches:** Making the next decision better than the last one, on purpose, is the reason a
  process exists. Writing a lesson down as *I have learned that…* keeps it.
- **Behind it:** *a gap*; no paper.

#### Q16 · What you'd need to keep it
- *Asked at:* Researching.
- *Lands under:* **How I decide**.
- **Ask:** What would you need (knowledge, tools, time, people) to follow a process every week?
- **Plain:** What would make it easy to stick to a plan every week?
- **Example:** "An hour on Sundays, and one place where all my data lives."
- **Teaches:** A process you can't keep is a plan, not a process.
- **Behind it:** *a gap*; no paper. For orientation only: Act VII, problem 4, research that runs
  itself, with Guo, Wang, Ni & Shum, 2022, *Quant 4.0: Engineering Quantitative Investment with
  Automated, Explainable and Knowledge-driven AI* (p. 53).
