# Momentum Crashes

*The whole paper, pages 1–57 of the PDF, from Luna's library, which read it whole: the eight
sections that bear on claim 5. The others — hedging the market with future and past betas, variance
risk and the size and value factors in panic states, momentum's own volatility, and the other equity
markets and asset classes, with the dynamic strategy applied to them — are not carried. Page numbers
are the PDF's own pages; from p. 4 on they run three ahead of the paper's printed numbers.*

## Why it is here

Claim 5 — momentum among the most traded — and Experiment 4, its second design. After Experiment 3's
book of the twenty with the highest twelve-month return trailed the pool's twenty most traded and
failed its kill switch, the owner chose on 2026-09-25 to hold momentum's twenty only outside a bear
market, a negative return of the index over the prior 24 months, and the pool's twenty most traded
inside one. This paper is where that state comes from. It was carried here after Experiment 3
reported, which saw the 2009 rebound the paper describes.

## Momentum pays across markets and asset classes, and no explanation of it is accepted

- "A momentum strategy is a bet that past returns will predict future returns in the cross-section
  of assets, and is typically implemented by buying past winners and selling past losers"; it is
  documented across time periods, markets and asset classes "that include equities, bonds,
  currencies, commodities, and exchange-traded futures" (p. 4).
- The history the authors cite: first documented in US stocks over 1965 to 1989 by Jegadeesh and
  Titman (1993) and Asness (1995); continuing over 1990 to 1998 (Jegadeesh and Titman 2001); robust
  over 1927 to 1965 and 1990 to 2012 (Israel and Moskowitz 2013); and over 1801 to 2012 in what
  Geczy and Samonov (2013) call "the world's longest backtest" (p. 4, footnote 1).
- "the underlying mechanism responsible for its returns is as yet unknown": the Sharpe ratios are
  hard to reconcile with rational-expectations pricing (Hansen and Jagannathan 1991), and the lack
  of correlation with proxies for macroeconomic risk "sharpens the puzzle still further" (p. 4).
- Behavioural models purport to produce momentum, "but no single theory has been accepted as a
  definitive explanation" (p. 5).

> **For claim 5:** the paper takes momentum's existence as settled, as Baltussen et al. do; what it
> adds is when the premium fails. Neither gives this claim a mechanism to rest on: this paper finds
> none accepted (p. 5), and the section on explanations below contradicts the risk-based reading the
> Paleologo chapter note records.

## The 1947–2006 record is strong, and the window was chosen to leave out the crashes

- Construction: CRSP common shares on the NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ, ranked on the cumulative return
  from month t − 12 to t − 2, skipping the latest month "to avoid the short-term one-month
  reversals", with at least eight valid returns in the past eleven months; ten value-weighted
  deciles rebalanced monthly; WML is the winner decile minus the loser decile (pp. 9–10).
- "This particular 60-year period is chosen purposely because it avoids the turbulent
  great-depression and WW-II periods before 1947, and the great recession after 2007", and its
  cumulative plot assumes "no transaction costs" (p. 10).
- Over 1947:01–2006:12 the winner decile's excess return averages 16.0 percent a year and the loser
  decile's −6.1, against 7.5 for the market. WML has a Sharpe ratio of 1.08 against the market's
  0.52, a beta of −0.25 and a CAPM alpha of 28.6 percent a year (t = 9.0) (p. 11).
- Winners are "considerably more negatively skewed" than losers "even over this relatively benign
  period"; WML's skewness is −2.01 monthly and −0.60 daily (pp. 11–12, Table 1).

> **For claim 5:** a momentum record that leaves out the crises reads a Sharpe ratio of 1.08, and
> over 1927–2013 it reads 0.71 (next section). Experiment 4's window, 2002-07-30 to 2026-06-01,
> holds the 2001–2002 and 2009 months the paper counts among its fifteen worst, so its figures are
> of the full-sample kind, not the calm kind.

## Over 1927–2013 crashes are rare, clustered and slow, and the fifteen worst follow a fallen market

- Over 1927:01–2013:03 WML earns 21.3 percent a year at a volatility of 30.1, with an alpha of 25.7
  (t = 8.5) and a beta of −0.58; its Sharpe ratio is 0.71 against the market's 0.40, and its
  skewness −4.70 monthly and −1.18 daily (p. 15, Table 2). "the Sharpe ratio and alpha understate
  the significance of these crashes" (p. 13).
- The two largest sustained drawdowns: from June 1932 to December 1939 the losers outperformed the
  winners by 50 percent, and from March 8, 2009 to March 28, 2013 "the losers produce more than
  twice the profits as the winners" (p. 12).
- "Every one of the 15 worst momentum returns occurs when the lagged two-year market return is
  negative. The worst 14 occur in months where the market rose, often in a dramatic fashion"
  (p. 15). The worst four are August 1932, −74.36 percent, July 1932, −60.98, January 2001, −49.19,
  and April 2009, −45.52; seven of the fifteen fall in 2001–2002 and 2009 (p. 16, Table 3).
- "a crash is not a Poisson jump. They take place slowly, over the span of multiple months". The two
  worst months are back to back, after "a market decline of roughly 90% from the 1929 peak", and
  three of the ten worst are from 2009 (p. 15).
- The asymmetry is in the tail: "the extreme momentum gains are not nearly as large in magnitude, or
  as concentrated in time" (p. 15).
- Of the sample's 1,035 months, 183 are bear-market months by the paper's definition, a negative
  market return over the prior 24 months (p. 20, footnote 9).

> **For claim 5:** this is the state Experiment 4 conditions on. Every one of the fifteen worst
> momentum months came when the market's return over the prior 24 months was negative, a number
> known at the month's start. The paper's months are long-short, on deciles of every listed common
> share; this book holds twenty long names among the hundred most traded, so what the state does to
> a long-only book is not measured here. The analyzer, before the blueprint, and the experiment
> measure it.

## The crashes come from the short side: after a fall, past losers are high-beta and option-like

- In July and August 1932 the market rose 82 percent, the winner decile 30 and the loser decile 236;
  from March to May 2009 the market rose 29 percent and the losers 156, so the losers were "crashing
  up" (p. 15). The introduction gives 232 and 32 percent for 1932, and 163 for the losers and 8 for
  the winners in 2009 (p. 5).
- At the start of March 2009 the loser decile was down 84 percent from its peak on average and held
  "Citigroup, Bank of America, Ford, GM, and International Paper"; the winners were "defensive or
  counter-cyclical firms like Autozone"; in the sense of Merton (1990) the losers' stock was
  "effectively an out-of-the-money option on the underlying firm value" (p. 16).
- The mechanism is the ranking-period market return (Kothari and Shanken 1992; Grundy and Martin
  2001): after a fall, the firms that fell with the market are high-beta and the best performers
  low-beta (pp. 5–6).
- On 126-day rolling regressions of daily returns with ten lags of the market, "following major
  market declines, betas for the past-loser decile rise above 3, and fall below 0.5 for past
  winners" (p. 6); the winners' beta "is sometimes above 2 following large market rises", while the
  losers' "reach far higher levels" (p. 18). The lagged coefficients are strongly significant for
  the losers, especially before the war (p. 18, footnote 8).
- In a conditional CAPM, WML's beta is lower by 1.136 in bear markets (t = −13.4) (p. 21).

> **For claim 5:** the crash is in the losers this book never holds, but the same mechanism reaches
> its long leg. After a fall the past winners are low-beta, defensive names, with betas below 0.5
> against the losers' above 3 (p. 6), and a rebound leaves them behind. Experiment 3 recorded
> exactly that: its twenty gained 29.14% from 2009-03-02 to 2009-12-31, against the most traded
> twenty's 65.93%. This note was brought here after that result, and Experiment 4's blueprint says
> so.

## In bear markets momentum behaves like a written call on the market, and only in bear markets

- The bear-market indicator is 1 when the CRSP value-weighted return over the 24 months before the
  month is negative; the up-market indicator is contemporaneous, 1 "if the excess CRSP VW index
  return is greater than the risk-free rate in month t" (p. 20).
- Unconditionally WML's beta is −0.577 and its intercept 1.9 percent a month (t = 7.3); with the
  bear indicator, the bear-market beta is lower by 1.136 (t = −13.4) and the bear-market alpha is
  "−1 basis point per month" (p. 21).
- A Henriksson and Merton (1981) regression separates up- and down-market betas: "in a bear market,
  a momentum portfolio's up-market beta is more than double its down-market beta (−1.51 versus
  −0.70), and that this difference is highly statistically significant (t-stat = 4.5). Outside of
  bear markets, there is no statistically significant difference in betas" (p. 6). Section 2.6 gives
  −0.74 and −1.44 for the same comparison (p. 22), and the extract loses Table 4's column alignment
  in its bear-market rows, so which regression each pair comes from cannot be read off (p. 21).
- The losers drive it: in bear markets the loser decile's down-market beta is 1.568 and its
  up-market beta 2.164, the winners' up-market increment is −0.214, and the difference shrinks
  monotonically toward the middle deciles (pp. 22–23, Table 5).
- "momentum strategies, in bear markets, behave like written calls on the market: When the market
  falls, they gain a little; when the market rises they lose dramatically" (p. 7).
- In bull markets "no significant asymmetry is present in the loser portfolio", the winners' is
  comparable to the bear-market case, and "the WML portfolio shows no statistically significant
  optionality in bull markets" (pp. 23–24, Table 6).

> **For claim 5:** in a bear state momentum's payoff is asymmetric against a rising market, and
> outside it the paper finds no significant asymmetry (pp. 23–24). A rule that uses the ranking only
> outside bear states keeps the months where the paper finds the premium and gives up the months
> where it finds a written call. Whether the long leg alone earns enough in the kept months to beat
> the most traded, net, is what the claim asks.

## A bear-market indicator and market variance forecast low momentum returns

- The ex-ante market variance is the variance of daily market returns over the 126 days before the
  month (p. 25).
- "both estimated market variance and the bear market indicator independently forecast future
  momentum returns": "in periods of high market stress, as indicated by bear markets and high
  volatility, momentum returns are low", and "particularly poor during bear markets with high
  volatility" (p. 26).
- The extract prints Table 7's coefficients without their column positions, so its five
  specifications cannot be told apart (p. 25).
- The authors place the result beside Cooper, Gutierrez and Hameed (2004), where the premium falls
  to zero after a negative three-year market return, and Stivers and Sun (2010), where it is low
  when market volatility is high (p. 5, footnote 4).

> **For claim 5:** two month-start variables forecast low momentum returns, the bear indicator and
> the market's trailing variance, and the two together worst. Experiment 4 uses the first alone, as
> the owner chose; the variance is a lever a later experiment would have to earn. Cooper, Gutierrez
> and Hameed (2004), whom the paper cites (p. 5, footnote 4), find the premium falling to zero after
> a negative three-year market return: a lead, not a note.

## Weighting momentum by forecast mean over forecast variance more than doubles its Sharpe ratio

- Appendix B: with normal returns, costless trading and conditional means and variances known in
  advance ("at t = 0 the agent knows µt and σt"), the weight that maximises a strategy's full-period
  Sharpe ratio is "proportional to the expected excess return over the next period, and inversely
  proportional to the conditional variance", times a time-invariant constant (pp. 26, 48–49). The
  equations of section 3 and the appendix are partly garbled in the extract; the statement in words
  is not.
- The rule sets the scaled strategy's conditional volatility "proportional to the conditional Sharpe
  ratio" (p. 7). With a constant Sharpe ratio it would be a constant-volatility rule like that of
  Barroso and Santa-Clara (2012), who argue that scaling by trailing volatility avoids the crashes
  (p. 27); for momentum the ratio is not constant, since "the return of WML is slightly negatively
  related to the forecast of WML return volatility" (p. 27).
- Inputs: the mean from the bear-market indicator times the market's trailing six-month variance,
  "as estimated in the last column of Table 7", a regression over 1927:07–2013:03; the variance from
  a GJR-GARCH on daily WML returns, fitted "over the full time series", combined with the trailing
  126-day volatility (pp. 25–27).
- The objective is "maximizing the in-sample unconditional Sharpe ratio" (p. 26); the scale is
  chosen so that "the in-sample annualized volatility of the strategy is 19%" (p. 28); in the
  figures the constant-volatility comparison is "also scaled ex-post" to 19 percent (p. 30, footnote
  11); the subsamples use "the same mean and variance forecasting equation and the same calibration"
  (p. 29). The abstract calls the strategy "implementable" (p. 3).
- Sharpe ratios, static, constant-volatility and dynamic: 1927:01–2013:03, 0.60, 1.01 and 1.18, with
  monthly skewness of −4.70, −0.76 and 0.09; 1927–1949, 0.27, 0.63, 0.68; 1950–1974, 1.31, 1.51,
  1.67; 1975–1999, 1.48, 1.69, 1.85; 2000:01–2013:03, 0.27, 0.59, 0.96 (p. 30, Table 8). The
  ordering holds in every subsample, and "even over sub-periods devoid of those crashes, there is
  still some improvement" (p. 30).
- The optimal portfolio's volatility "is lowest when WML's volatility is forecast to be high (and
  it's mean return low)" (p. 28).
- Table 8's full-period static Sharpe ratio, 0.60, differs from Table 2's 0.71 for the same stated
  period, and the paper does not say why (pp. 15, 30).
- The text calls the outperformance significant (p. 7), but Tables 8 and 13 print no standard error
  or test for any Sharpe ratio or difference (pp. 30, 45).
- "These calculated returns do not incorporate transaction costs" (pp. 47–48); no turnover is
  reported for any of the three strategies.

> **For claim 5:** the headline doubling is in sample, before costs, with forecasts fitted on the
> whole period it is scored on, and no test is printed (pp. 26–30, 47–48): it is not evidence for a
> book. Experiment 4 takes only the bear indicator, a fixed rule with no fitted coefficient, and
> charges its costs. The constant-volatility column, sturdier because it needs no forecast of the
> mean, is a scaling this book does not use.

## No risk explanation accounts for the findings, and a behavioural one is left open

- "in extreme market environments following a long market downturn, the market prices of past losers
  embody a very high premium" (p. 45); the losers' optionality "does not appear to generally be
  reflected in the prices of the past losers. Consequently, the expected returns of the past losers
  are very high, and the momentum effect is reversed during these times" (p. 46).
- The authors examine explanations "ranging from compensation for crash risk to volatility risk, to
  other factor risks such as the Fama and French (1993) factors, but find that none of these
  explanations can account fully for our findings, though volatility risk goes in the right
  direction" (p. 46).
- A Merton story "may make sense" for equity momentum, but the same option-like features in futures,
  bonds, currencies and commodities make it "more challenging" (p. 46); the introduction calls "a
  fuller understanding of these convex payoffs" "an open area for future research" (p. 8).
- The behavioural alternative: "in extreme situations individuals tend to be fearful and appear to
  focus on losses, largely ignoring probabilities", citing Sunstein and Zeckhauser (2008),
  Loewenstein, Weber, Hsee and Welch (2001) and Loewenstein (2000); whether it fits "is a subject
  for further research" (p. 46).
- The dynamic results "pose a significantly greater challenge for rational asset pricing models"
  than the static strategy (p. 8).

> **For claim 5:** the crash-state premium is not compensation for market, variance, size or value
> risk on the paper's tests (p. 46), which contradicts the risk-based reading of the Paleologo
> chapter note; a warning there now names this note. What the ranking earns outside bear states is
> left with a behavioural story, untested here and not claimed by the objective.

## What it changes

- For claim 5, gives Experiment 4 its state: a negative return of the market over the prior 24
  months, after which all fifteen of the paper's worst momentum months came, and in which past
  winners are low-beta and lag a rebound.
- Says what a momentum record over a window holding a bear market and its rebound should look like:
  lower than a calm window's, and still positive in the paper's long-short sample.
- Leaves open whether the state helps a long-only book of twenty liquid names, net: the paper's
  evidence is long-short deciles, gross of costs. The analyzer's split by state, before the
  blueprint, and Experiment 4 measure it.
- Its dynamic strategy is not evidence for a book: in sample, before costs, with no test printed.
